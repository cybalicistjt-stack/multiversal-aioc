# ENV-06 — Freshwater & Wetland Preset Content

**Program:** ENV — Environment Preset & Overlay  
**Tranche:** ENV-06 — Freshwater & Wetland Expansion  
**Authority class:** owner-authorized new content, not recovered source text  
**Application implementation authority:** none

## Scope and authorship boundary

This package adds six GM-ready freshwater/wetland presets under the ENV composition model. It does not rewrite the forty retained source profiles. The material below is newly authored under the owner-approved ENV-06 program and must not later be misrepresented as text recovered from the historical environment PDFs.

Each preset supplies the current minimum environment content classes: overview, environmental features, movement/navigation, hazards, encounters/challenges, rest/shelter, and a d12 random encounter table. These are preset-level content deltas and guidance used with inherited archetype behavior; they are not a return to isolated comprehensive environment rulebooks.

The encounter tables intentionally use ecological roles and environmental events rather than declaring canonical creature identities or world distribution. CEW owns those later mappings. Exact Habitat Signature vocabulary remains ENV-15. Concrete weather, planetary/physical, and supernatural overlay definitions remain ENV-11/12/13.

## 1. River / Stream

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-RIVER-STREAM`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-FLOWING-WATER`  
**Variants:** River; Stream

### Overview

A channelized freshwater corridor defined by directional current, banks, a bed, crossings, tributaries, bends, bars, pools, shallows and deeper runs. A stream is the smaller/narrower expression; a river is broader and more likely to support sustained vessel travel, major crossings and large floodplains. Exact width, depth and current strength are local parameters rather than separate environment identities.

### Environmental features

- banks ranging from firm shelves to mud, roots, reeds, rock or cut embankments;
- riffles, pools, runs, rapids, falls, bars, islands and debris accumulations as local channel features;
- tributaries and side channels that create route choices and orientation landmarks;
- exposed shallows and fords that can become strategic crossing points;
- riparian vegetation and bank cover distinct from the surrounding biome;
- current direction as a persistent navigation fact even when flow strength changes.

### Movement and navigation

- upstream and downstream travel are inherently asymmetric;
- crossing the channel is different from traveling along it and may depend on ford, bridge, ferry, swimming, flotation or local equipment owned by other systems;
- bank-to-water access can be easy, steep, muddy, concealed or blocked;
- rapids, falls, snags, bars and constrictions create route hazards without requiring a separate environment preset;
- the river itself can act as a navigation corridor while bends, tributaries and braided sections complicate orientation.

### Hazards

- strong or deceptive current;
- submerged obstacles and unstable footing;
- steep or collapsing banks;
- sudden depth changes and concealed channels;
- cold-water exposure where locally applicable;
- contamination or dangerous water quality only when supplied by source/local facts or later overlays;
- exceptional flooding remains an overlay/current-state issue rather than being assumed in the baseline preset.

### Encounters and challenges

Use crossing problems, blocked channels, changing banks, lost or damaged crossings, signs of wildlife activity, fishing/foraging opportunities, debris, stranded travelers, hidden side channels, territorial use of water access, and local settlement/route interactions. Canonical creatures are supplied later by CEW and World distribution authority.

### Rest and shelter

Banks above ordinary high-water level, dry gravel bars when safely established, tree cover, rock shelves, bridgeside structures, boats or nearby built shelters may provide rest opportunities. Low islands, undercut banks and apparently dry channels can become unsafe when water level changes.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A usually easy ford is deeper or faster than expected.
2. Fresh tracks, feeding signs or disturbed vegetation converge on the bank.
3. A jam of branches and debris partially blocks the channel.
4. A quiet side channel offers a shortcut but poor visibility.
5. A damaged bridge, ferry point or improvised crossing demands a decision.
6. A school or concentration of aquatic life creates a sudden feeding disturbance.
7. Eroding bank material collapses or reveals something previously buried.
8. A shallow bar or island provides temporary footing and an exposed vantage point.
9. Upstream activity sends floating debris or discoloration downstream.
10. A narrow rapid or constriction forces careful route choice.
11. Travelers, workers or locals are using the same crossing or water access.
12. The channel divides around an island and the branches differ sharply in speed, depth or concealment.

## 2. Lake / Pond

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-LAKE-POND`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-OPEN-WATER`  
**Variants:** Lake; Pond

### Overview

Standing inland freshwater ranging from small ponds with shore-dominated play to large lakes where open-water navigation, weather exposure and depth become major factors. Size is a preset parameter: a pond does not require a separate archetype, and a large lake can behave like open water while remaining freshwater and geographically enclosed.

### Environmental features

- shoreline shallows grading into deeper water;
- coves, inlets, outlets, reed beds, floating vegetation, islands or exposed rock where locally present;
- thermally or visibly distinct surface/deep zones without assuming a finalized Habitat Signature;
- soft mud, sand, gravel, rock or vegetation-dense shore substrates;
- isolated water access points in steep, wooded or built shorelines;
- seasonal water-level marks and exposed littoral zones.

### Movement and navigation

- small ponds emphasize shore access, wading and short crossings;
- large lakes may require swimming, vessels or other traversal support owned by external systems;
- distant shorelines, fog, glare and open water can impair orientation;
- islands, coves and peninsulas create route choices and sheltered water;
- entering or leaving the water may be easier than moving through dense shoreline vegetation or steep banks.

### Hazards

- unexpected drop-offs and soft bottoms;
- cold or deep water;
- wind-driven waves on large lakes;
- poor visibility beneath the surface;
- entangling vegetation or submerged debris;
- thin ice is not baseline and requires later cold/hydrology authority;
- contamination and magical effects require explicit local/source/overlay authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use shore-access disputes, stranded or drifting objects, islands, fishing/foraging activity, changing visibility, signs of aquatic or shoreline wildlife, submerged features, damaged docks or boats, and sudden changes between shallow and deep water. CEW later supplies actual creature lists.

### Rest and shelter

Sheltered coves, elevated shore, islands, boathouses, docks, caves or nearby woodland may offer rest depending on the local instance. Exposed shorelines and low islands provide little protection from wind or rising water.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A patch of floating vegetation conceals an obstacle or point of interest.
2. Ripples or feeding activity spread across otherwise still water.
3. A shoreline trail ends at an unexpectedly steep or marshy section.
4. A drifting object approaches from open water.
5. A small island or rock outcrop offers shelter but signs show recent use.
6. Sudden wind roughens the open surface while sheltered coves remain calm.
7. Water clarity changes sharply over a drop-off or submerged shelf.
8. Tracks and repeated paths reveal a heavily used watering point.
9. A dock, landing or boat is damaged, abandoned or poorly secured.
10. A shallow inlet offers resources but difficult footing and dense cover.
11. Something below the surface disturbs sediment or vegetation without immediate identification.
12. The safest route requires choosing between a long shoreline detour and a direct water crossing.

## 3. Floodplain

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-FLOODPLAIN`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-WETLAND`  
**Secondary archetypes:** `ARCH-OPEN-COUNTRY`, `ARCH-FLOWING-WATER`

### Overview

Low terrain adjacent to a river or river system, shaped by recurring inundation, deposited sediment, old channels, levees, backwaters and alternating wet/dry conditions. The preset describes floodplain geography even when no active flood is occurring. A current extreme flood is a later hydrology/weather overlay or runtime state.

### Environmental features

- natural levees, low basins, oxbows, abandoned channels and backwaters;
- alternating firm ground, silt, mud, shallow water and vegetated low spots;
- river access interwoven with broad open terrain;
- visible flood marks, debris lines and sediment deposits;
- seasonal or recurrent changes in usable routes and resources;
- fertile soils and concentrated water access where the local world supports them.

### Movement and navigation

- apparently open terrain can be cut by old channels, drainage lines and soft ground;
- raised levees and roads can become preferred corridors;
- watercourses may shift or multiply after high-water periods;
- long sightlines coexist with local concealment in reeds, brush, channels and embankments;
- crossings and dry routes depend strongly on current water level.

### Hazards

- mud, hidden channels and unstable banks;
- isolated pockets of deep or stagnant water;
- stranded debris and damaged infrastructure after high water;
- sudden loss of a dry route when water rises;
- exposure on broad open ground;
- active flood force, severe storms, contamination or disease effects require later overlay/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use stranded materials, washed-out routes, fresh sediment revealing or burying features, wildlife concentrated near shrinking water, travelers seeking high ground, temporary pools, cut-off settlements, changed crossings and competing use of dry levees or resources.

### Rest and shelter

Natural levees, elevated ground, sturdy trees, raised structures and established embankments are preferable to low basins. A campsite that is dry now is not automatically safe from later water rise; the GM should distinguish ordinary floodplain geography from an active flood overlay.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A dry-looking depression proves to be deep mud or shallow standing water.
2. A former channel creates an unexpected obstacle across the route.
3. Flood debris marks a recent high-water line well above current water.
4. Tracks and feeding signs concentrate around an isolated pool.
5. A raised levee offers fast travel but little concealment.
6. A washed-out crossing forces a detour toward higher ground.
7. Fresh sediment has exposed an object, ruin or resource patch.
8. A backwater channel provides cover and a possible alternate route.
9. Local travelers or workers are moving supplies along the same dry corridor.
10. A cluster of stranded aquatic life attracts scavenging activity.
11. A seemingly solid bank gives way under weight.
12. The river has recently shifted enough that an old map or remembered route is misleading.

## 4. River Delta / Estuary

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-DELTA-ESTUARY`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-FLOWING-WATER`  
**Secondary archetypes:** `ARCH-WETLAND`, `ARCH-COASTAL`

### Overview

A branching river-mouth environment where channels, wetlands, bars, islands and coastal interface create a complex transition between inland flow and larger receiving waters. The `river_delta` variant emphasizes distributary channels and deposited landforms. The `estuary` variant emphasizes mixed freshwater/brackish conditions and coastal exchange. Detailed tidal flats, marine ecology, surf/coast behavior and other marine-specific refinement remain ENV-07.

### Environmental features

- branching channels and distributaries;
- low islands, bars, mudflats or vegetated banks where locally applicable;
- rapid changes between navigable channel, shallow water and saturated land;
- mixed freshwater/brackish transition in estuarine instances;
- sediment deposition and shifting channel edges;
- multiple routes whose usefulness changes with water level and later tidal authority.

### Movement and navigation

- route choice matters because channels split, reconnect, shoal or terminate in wetlands;
- land travel is interrupted by frequent waterways and soft ground;
- water travel depends on identifying navigable channels rather than merely following downstream flow;
- bank access may be difficult where reeds, mud, roots or unstable sediment dominate;
- coastal/tidal timing mechanics are deferred to ENV-07/11/12 rather than invented here.

### Hazards

- disorientation in branching channels;
- soft sediment, mud and hidden shallows;
- current convergence or divergence;
- stranded travel routes as water levels change;
- poor potable-water assumptions in brackish zones;
- storm surge, strong tides, marine predators and contamination require later or CEW authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use navigation decisions, shifting channels, mixed-water resource questions, stranded craft or cargo, fishing/harvesting activity, wildlife concentrated on bars and channel edges, concealed camps, competing use of navigable passages and signs that a known channel has changed.

### Rest and shelter

Higher islands, established structures and vegetation-rooted banks can offer shelter. Low bars and exposed mudflats are unreliable rest sites. Estuary-specific tide safety remains a later coastal/overlay concern.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. The main-looking channel becomes too shallow while a narrower branch remains navigable.
2. A newly deposited bar divides the current around exposed sediment.
3. Dense vegetation conceals a narrow passage between channels.
4. Water taste, color or salinity shifts noticeably across a mixing zone.
5. Feeding activity concentrates where two currents meet.
6. A stranded vessel, platform or cargo rests on mud or a shallow bar.
7. A low island shows signs of temporary occupation or repeated animal use.
8. The route opens suddenly into a broad exposed water junction.
9. A bank collapses and clouds the water with fresh sediment.
10. Local travelers insist a familiar channel has moved since the last season.
11. A maze of shallow channels creates several plausible but unequal routes.
12. An inland route and a water route converge at a valuable high-ground crossing point.

## 5. Marsh / Bog / Fen

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-MARSH-BOG-FEN`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-WETLAND`  
**Variants:** Marsh; Bog; Fen

### Overview

A wetland family expressed through subtype parameters rather than three duplicated environment systems. **Marsh** emphasizes shallow water and herbaceous vegetation. **Bog** emphasizes peat, acidic/nutrient-poor conditions and precipitation-fed water. **Fen** emphasizes peat/wet ground influenced by groundwater or mineral-rich flow. A setting may alter those real-world-like assumptions explicitly; these are authored baseline distinctions, not claims about every Multiversal wetland.

### Environmental features

- saturated soil, shallow standing water and irregular firm ground;
- channels, pools, hummocks, mats and low ridges that create micro-routes;
- subtype-specific vegetation structure rather than a universal dense forest canopy;
- peat or organic soil in bog/fen variants;
- visible water may not indicate reliable depth or footing;
- open sightlines can coexist with dense low vegetation and poor ground-level visibility.

### Movement and navigation

- firm-looking surfaces may be soft, floating or discontinuous;
- shallow-water travel, wading and detours around pools are common;
- hummocks, ridges, boardwalks or known local paths become valuable routes;
- direction can be difficult to maintain in visually repetitive open wetlands;
- boats or specialized equipment, where used, remain owned by external systems.

### Hazards

- deep mud, concealed pools and unstable organic ground;
- difficult extraction after a fall or misstep;
- poor visibility at ground/water level;
- limited dry shelter;
- water chemistry or contamination can make apparently abundant water unusable;
- disease, toxic gas, supernatural bog effects and extreme weather require explicit local/overlay authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use lost trails, isolated firm ground, signs of burrowing/wading/aerial wildlife, resource patches, hidden pools, abandoned walkways, difficult rescues, localized water chemistry differences and navigation by sparse landmarks. CEW later replaces generic wildlife roles with eligible creatures.

### Rest and shelter

Dry hummocks, raised ridges, constructed platforms, isolated tree stands or local structures can serve as rest sites. The GM should track whether a chosen site is actually firm and above ordinary water rather than treating any visible land as safe.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A mat of vegetation flexes under weight and conceals deeper water.
2. A narrow firm ridge provides unexpectedly fast travel through the wetland.
3. Repeated tracks converge on a small dry hummock.
4. An apparently shallow pool has no immediately visible bottom.
5. A patch of water differs sharply in color, clarity or chemistry from nearby pools.
6. A damaged boardwalk, causeway or old marker line crosses part of the area.
7. Low vegetation suddenly erupts with fleeing or feeding wildlife activity.
8. A dense patch of reeds or sedges conceals a sheltered pocket.
9. The easiest route leads into increasingly soft ground and must be reconsidered.
10. A peat or mud bank has slumped, exposing preserved material or a hidden cavity.
11. Distant landmarks become obscured, making direction harder to maintain.
12. A localized patch of firm ground shows signs of repeated occupation, nesting or camp use.

## 6. Flooded Forest

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-FW-FLOODED-FOREST`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-FOREST`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-WETLAND`

### Overview

A forest whose normal or recurrent baseline includes shallow standing or slow-moving freshwater, saturated soil and tree-dominated structure. This is distinct from applying an exceptional `Flood` condition to an ordinary forest: the vegetation, routes, shelter patterns and local ecology are assumed to be shaped by recurrent or persistent water. Temporary catastrophic flooding remains a later overlay/runtime state.

### Environmental features

- trunks, roots and canopy combined with shallow water, pools and saturated soil;
- natural channels, submerged trails and raised root masses or islands;
- fallen timber that may act as bridge, barrier or concealed hazard;
- alternating open water under canopy and dense understory pockets;
- tree-based navigation landmarks with poor ground visibility;
- seasonal water variation may be part of the local preset parameters.

### Movement and navigation

- terrestrial routes break into wading, root-walking, log crossings and short dry patches;
- small watercraft may fit some channels but not dense tree spacing;
- underwater roots and debris complicate movement;
- sightlines are limited by both forest structure and reflective/vegetated water surfaces;
- known raised routes, old trails and channel networks become important navigation aids.

### Hazards

- submerged roots, holes and debris;
- deep channels hidden among shallow water;
- falling or unstable deadwood;
- difficult footing during transitions between water, mud and roots;
- limited dry rest areas;
- active flood current, contamination, wildfire, extreme weather or supernatural effects require later overlay/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use blocked channels, wildlife activity in canopy/water interfaces, stranded dry-ground resources, fallen trees forming crossings, concealed camps, changed trails, water-level marks, damaged structures and route choices between dense forest and deeper water.

### Rest and shelter

Raised root systems, dry islands, tree platforms, sturdy structures and established high-ground routes provide the best shelter. A site may offer overhead canopy protection while still being unsuitable for ground rest because of standing water.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A fallen tree forms a useful crossing but is slick, unstable or heavily used by wildlife.
2. A submerged trail is visible only as a line of clearer water between trunks.
3. Feeding or movement activity shifts suddenly from the water to the canopy.
4. A dry island holds signs of repeated resting, nesting or camp use.
5. A hidden deeper channel cuts across an otherwise wadeable route.
6. Root tangles block a water passage but conceal a sheltered pocket beyond.
7. Water marks on trunks show a recent level substantially above the current surface.
8. A partially submerged structure or object appears between the trees.
9. Floating debris accumulates against trunks and creates a temporary barrier.
10. A raised old trail offers fast movement but exposes travelers between trees.
11. A cluster of dead or damaged trees creates both open sightlines and falling hazards.
12. The route divides between shallow obstructed water and a longer muddy path on marginally higher ground.

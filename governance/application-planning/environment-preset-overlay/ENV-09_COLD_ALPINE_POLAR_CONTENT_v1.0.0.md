# ENV-09 — Cold, Alpine & Polar Preset Content

**Program:** ENV — Environment Preset & Overlay  
**Tranche:** ENV-09 — Cold, Alpine & Polar Expansion  
**Authority class:** owner-authorized new content derived from the approved ENV program, not recovered source text  
**Application implementation authority:** none

## Scope and authorship boundary

This package adds six modular cold/alpine/polar presets while preserving the existing source-backed `Arctic Tundra and Taiga` preset as immutable provenance. The new presets do not replace, rewrite or claim to be recovered text from that source profile. They provide reusable modern ENV compositions that separate forest, open-country, highland and persistent-ice structures.

Generic cold severity, Extreme Cold, blizzard/whiteout, active avalanche, low oxygen, unusual pressure, prolonged darkness/illumination and other transient or planetary conditions remain later overlay/local-state authority under ENV-11/12. Encounter entries use ecological roles, signs and environmental events rather than declaring canonical creatures; CEW owns those mappings. Habitat Signature vocabulary remains ENV-15.

## 1. Taiga / Boreal Forest

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-TAIGA`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-FOREST`

### Overview

A cold-region forest dominated by persistent tree cover, long travel corridors, patchy openings, boggy or frozen low ground, and strong seasonal variation. Its defining structure is still Forest: cold and snow can intensify it, but they are not what makes it an environment identity.

### Environmental features

- dense or semi-open coniferous woodland with occasional mixed growth;
- lakes, streams, boggy hollows and windthrow openings where locally present;
- heavy ground cover, roots, fallen timber and uneven forest floor;
- tree cover that can reduce wind while also obscuring distant landmarks;
- seasonal snowpack, frozen ground or thawed wet patches only where current conditions support them.

### Movement and navigation

Travel follows forest routes, ridges, waterways, game trails and clearings. Snow or ice can alter speed and traction but are not assumed universally. Dense stands can force detours, while frozen waterways may become temporary corridors only when later local/overlay authority establishes safe ice.

### Hazards

Falling limbs, concealed holes, unstable snow-laden branches, boggy thaw zones, thin ice over small water, poor visibility and route-finding errors can occur. Extreme cold, blizzard conditions and wildfire require separate authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use tracks crossing trails, disturbed snow or litter, blocked passes through fallen timber, isolated camps, frozen or open water barriers, food/resource signs, distant calls, damaged shelters and evidence of migration or seasonal movement.

### Rest and shelter

Tree cover, dense stands, rock outcrops, snow-free ground beneath heavy canopy and existing shelters may reduce exposure. Deadfall zones, low wet ground and isolated trees in high wind are poor rest choices.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. Fresh tracks cross the route and disappear into dense cover.
2. Windthrow blocks the easiest trail through the trees.
3. A sheltered hollow is warmer but damp or soft underfoot.
4. A narrow frozen-looking stream interrupts the route.
5. Distant movement is glimpsed between trunks without clear identification.
6. A stand of dense trees provides strong shelter but poor visibility.
7. Broken branches and stripped bark mark recent animal activity.
8. A small clearing exposes travelers to wind and distant observation.
9. A forgotten camp or rough shelter shows signs of recent reuse.
10. A familiar landmark is obscured by dense growth or drifting snow.
11. A boggy depression is partly frozen and difficult to judge.
12. A long straight game trail offers speed but signs show frequent use by wildlife.

## 2. Tundra

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-TUNDRA`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-OPEN-COUNTRY`

### Overview

Treeless or nearly treeless open country with broad sightlines, sparse low vegetation, exposed terrain and limited natural shelter. Tundra can be frozen, snow-covered, wet during thaw, or comparatively dry; the preset describes the open ecological/terrain form rather than one permanent weather state.

### Environmental features

- broad low-relief terrain with mosses, grasses, lichens, dwarf shrubs or bare patches where appropriate;
- shallow drainage lines, ponds and thaw depressions;
- sparse shelter and few large vertical landmarks;
- ground that can range from firm/frozen to saturated and uneven depending on season;
- long visual horizons broken by low ridges, boulders or river cuts.

### Movement and navigation

Straight-line travel appears easy but may be disrupted by wet hollows, soft ground, shallow channels and hidden unevenness. Navigation depends heavily on distant terrain, sky and route markers. Strong wind or whiteout conditions are later overlays, not baseline.

### Hazards

Exposure, soft thawed ground, concealed water, frost-heaved terrain, few protected rest sites and long distances between resources can matter. Extreme Cold, severe wind and blizzard effects remain ENV-11/12.

### Encounters and challenges

Use migration signs, isolated watering points, route markers, distant moving groups, exposed remains, sudden ground changes, stranded travelers, temporary camps and resource concentrations.

### Rest and shelter

Low ridges, cut banks, boulder clusters, dug shelters and existing structures are valuable because natural cover is limited. Low depressions may block wind but can collect water or drifting snow.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A distant moving shape remains visible for a long time across open ground.
2. Tracks converge on a shallow water source.
3. A low ridge offers the first meaningful shelter in hours.
4. Firm ground gives way to a saturated thaw pocket.
5. An old marker or cairn points toward a safer route.
6. A broad patch of disturbed vegetation shows recent feeding activity.
7. A shallow channel forces a detour toward firmer crossing ground.
8. Remains exposed on open terrain attract scavenging activity.
9. A temporary camp occupies the only nearby sheltered depression.
10. Visibility is excellent, but there is nowhere nearby to hide.
11. Frost-heaved ground makes an apparently direct route exhausting.
12. A resource-rich patch attracts multiple kinds of wildlife sign.

## 3. Alpine / High Mountain

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-ALPINE`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-HIGHLAND`

### Overview

High-elevation terrain above or near the treeline where steep grades, exposed ridges, passes, cliffs, scree, rock shelves and limited sheltered ground dominate travel. Snow and cold are common possibilities but are not universal mechanical assumptions.

### Environmental features

- steep slopes, ridgelines, saddles and passes;
- exposed rock, scree, talus and sparse high-elevation vegetation;
- abrupt elevation transitions and restricted traversable corridors;
- sheltered bowls, ledges and valleys separated by exposed crossings;
- snowfields or ice patches where local conditions establish them.

### Movement and navigation

Routes are constrained by slope angle, cliffs and passable saddles. Small horizontal distances can require major elevation changes. High terrain gives strong visibility while increasing exposure. Equipment, climbing mechanics and physiological altitude rules remain externally owned.

### Hazards

Falls, loose rock, difficult traverses, exposed ridges, route dead ends and sudden weather exposure are baseline concerns. Avalanche, severe cold, low oxygen and storm effects require later overlay/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use blocked passes, unstable scree, distant movement on opposing slopes, high nests/dens, abandoned climbing routes, damaged bridges, isolated shelters, route-finding choices and evidence of rockfall or recent passage.

### Rest and shelter

Protected ledges, caves, lee slopes, alpine huts and sheltered bowls are valuable. Ridge crests and exposed saddles are poor rest sites unless conditions are unusually calm.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. The obvious pass is blocked by recent rockfall.
2. A narrow ledge offers the only route around a cliff band.
3. Distant movement appears on a parallel ridge.
4. Scree shifts underfoot and reveals a hidden drop.
5. An abandoned high shelter remains structurally usable.
6. A small spring or meltwater source appears below a rock face.
7. A route marker points toward a longer but safer descent.
8. Fresh debris shows that a slope recently failed.
9. A high overlook reveals several possible routes with different risks.
10. Tracks traverse a slope that seems almost too steep for easy travel.
11. A sheltered bowl contains signs of repeated seasonal use.
12. The shortest route requires crossing a highly exposed saddle.

## 4. Glacier / Icefield

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-GLACIER`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-ICE-MASS`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-HIGHLAND`

### Overview

A persistent land-ice mass or connected icefield whose surface is itself a moving, fractured terrain system. Glacier travel is not merely “mountain plus cold”: crevasses, seracs, melt channels, pressure ridges and shifting ice make the substrate a defining structural hazard.

### Environmental features

- crevasses, fractures, snow bridges and exposed blue ice;
- ridges, hummocks, seracs and compressed/deformed ice features;
- meltwater channels, pools and moulins where conditions permit;
- debris bands, moraine and rock carried or exposed by moving ice;
- calving or unstable margins on appropriate glacier forms.

### Movement and navigation

Safe routes may zigzag around fracture fields and steep ice. Surface appearance can conceal major voids. Glacier features move over time, so remembered routes and old maps can become unreliable. Exact load-bearing, rope, climbing and equipment mechanics remain external.

### Hazards

Crevasses, weak bridges, slips, falling ice, unstable margins, hidden meltwater and route collapse are intrinsic. Extreme Cold, whiteout, avalanche and low oxygen remain overlays/local conditions.

### Encounters and challenges

Use newly opened fractures, lost route markers, exposed objects or remains, isolated rock islands, meltwater barriers, unstable ice towers, stranded travelers and wildlife signs concentrated near margins or open water.

### Rest and shelter

Stable rock outcrops, protected moraine, established shelters and carefully evaluated ice hollows may offer rest. Crevasse edges, serac zones, drainage paths and calving margins are poor choices.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A narrow fracture has widened enough to block the known route.
2. A snow bridge crosses a crevasse but shows signs of weakening.
3. Dark debris on the ice reveals a firmer navigation line.
4. Meltwater cuts a channel across the intended path.
5. A rock outcrop provides the only secure shelter nearby.
6. A distant ice collapse echoes across the field.
7. Old markers lead toward a route that no longer exists.
8. Wind-polished ice creates a difficult exposed crossing.
9. An object or remains emerge from melting or fractured ice.
10. A pressure ridge forces a climb or long detour.
11. Fresh tracks approach the glacier margin from another terrain type.
12. A seemingly flat snow surface conceals irregular ice beneath.

## 5. Polar Ice

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-POLAR-ICE`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-ICE-MASS`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-OPEN-COUNTRY`

### Overview

A broad persistent land-ice environment such as an ice sheet, ice cap or extensive polar ice field, where immense open distances combine with frozen-surface structure. It differs from a valley glacier by scale and openness rather than by requiring another archetype.

### Environmental features

- huge open ice surfaces with few landmarks;
- wind-carved ridges, sastrugi-like surface relief and pressure features;
- fracture zones and crevasses near stressed terrain;
- exposed rock islands or nunatak-like features where locally present;
- melt ponds/channels or bare ice during suitable seasons.

### Movement and navigation

Travel can be physically direct yet navigationally difficult because distant features change slowly and surface detail repeats. Fracture zones or rough ice can make straight routes unsafe. Extreme lighting conditions, whiteout and severe wind remain later overlays.

### Hazards

Crevasses, weak ice zones, glare, lack of shelter, very long resource distances and disorientation are baseline structural concerns. Extreme Cold and prolonged darkness/daylight are not universal hard-coded mechanics.

### Encounters and challenges

Use distant shelters, navigation-marker failures, exposed debris, moving weather fronts, fracture fields, isolated rock, abandoned camps, long-range signals and wildlife sign around rare open-water or resource areas.

### Rest and shelter

Natural shelter is scarce. Rock outcrops, constructed shelters, vehicle/portable systems owned elsewhere and carefully selected ice depressions may provide protection.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A line of fractures forces a major course change.
2. A distant dark point resolves into rock, debris or a structure.
3. Repeating surface ridges make direction difficult to judge.
4. Tracks cross the route and continue toward the horizon.
5. A shallow melt feature interrupts otherwise solid travel.
6. Old route markers are partly buried or displaced.
7. A broad smooth ice area offers fast travel but little shelter.
8. A sudden elevation change reveals a hidden ice escarpment.
9. An abandoned cache or camp appears near an isolated landmark.
10. A crevasse field is visible only from a higher vantage.
11. Wind-carved ice creates a maze of small ridges and hollows.
12. A rare patch of exposed rock becomes a natural gathering point.

## 6. Sea Ice

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-CP-SEA-ICE`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-ICE-MASS`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-OPEN-WATER`

### Overview

Frozen marine surface ranging from relatively continuous pack ice to broken drift ice. Sea ice is simultaneously traversable ice and an ocean environment: cracks, leads, ridges and moving floes can turn apparent land travel into a mixed ice/water navigation problem.

### Environmental features

- floes, pack ice, leads and pressure ridges;
- cracks that open, close or shift over time;
- rough hummocked ice and smoother travel lanes;
- open-water edges that become ecological and navigational focal points;
- moving or rotating floes where local conditions support them.

### Movement and navigation

Routes must account for ice continuity, open leads and floe movement. A path safe at one time may separate later. Crossing water gaps requires external swimming/vessel/equipment systems. Exact ice thickness and carrying-capacity formulas are not authored here.

### Hazards

Weak ice, opening leads, floe separation, pressure-ridge collapse, unstable edges and immersion risk are intrinsic. Extreme cold, storms, fog and blizzard/whiteout remain overlays/local states.

### Encounters and challenges

Use moving leads, stranded equipment, open-water wildlife signs, separated travelers, pressure ridges, damaged camps, drifting debris, route markers that no longer align and changing access to the coast/open sea.

### Rest and shelter

Stable thick floes, pressure-ridge lee sides, constructed shelters and nearby land may provide rest. Floe edges, newly formed ice and active fracture zones are poor sites.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A narrow lead has opened across the intended route.
2. Pressure ridges create a rough wall of broken ice.
3. Tracks converge on an open-water edge.
4. A previously connected floe is slowly separating.
5. Drifting debris or equipment rests against the ice edge.
6. A broad smooth lane offers easier travel across the pack.
7. Cracking sounds signal movement somewhere nearby.
8. A small camp or shelter sits on a floe with uncertain stability.
9. Open water reveals movement beneath or beside the ice without clear identification.
10. A route marker has drifted far from its original alignment.
11. A jagged ridge provides shelter but difficult footing.
12. The safest continuation requires choosing between a long ice detour and a short water crossing.

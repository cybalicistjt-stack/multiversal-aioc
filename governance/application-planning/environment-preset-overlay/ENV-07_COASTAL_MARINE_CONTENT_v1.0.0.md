# ENV-07 — Coastal & Marine Preset Content

**Program:** ENV — Environment Preset & Overlay  
**Tranche:** ENV-07 — Coastal & Marine Expansion  
**Authority class:** owner-authorized new content, not recovered source text  
**Application implementation authority:** none

## Scope and authorship boundary

This package adds six GM-ready coastal/marine presets under the ENV composition model. It does not rewrite the forty retained source profiles or the six ENV-06 freshwater/wetland presets. The material below is newly authored under the owner-approved ENV-07 program and must not later be misrepresented as text recovered from historical PDFs.

Each preset supplies the minimum environment content classes: overview, environmental features, movement/navigation, hazards, encounters/challenges, rest/shelter, and a d12 random encounter table. The presets are modular content layered over reusable archetypes rather than isolated comprehensive rulesets.

Creature entries are deliberately expressed as ecological roles, signs, disturbances, or generic life activity. CEW owns canonical creature identities and world distribution. Exact Habitat Signature vocabulary remains ENV-15. Concrete tides, storms, pressure bands, temperature extremes, darkness, contamination, and supernatural effects remain later overlay/local authority where applicable.

## 1. Coast / Shoreline / Beach

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-COAST-SHORE`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-COASTAL`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-OPEN-COUNTRY`  
**Variants:** Coast; Shoreline; Beach

### Overview

The active boundary between land and a large body of water, ranging from broad beaches and low dunes to rocky shores, shelves, coves and narrow strand lines. The preset emphasizes changing access between land and water, exposure, shoreline orientation and the contrast between open ground and water-edge obstacles. Exact tidal range, surf intensity and storm state are later hydrology/weather parameters rather than fixed baseline assumptions.

### Environmental features

- sand, gravel, shell, rock shelf, cobble or mixed shore substrate;
- strand lines, wrack, drift material and wave-shaped debris deposits;
- coves, points, spits, bars and small inlets as local route features;
- dunes, low bluffs or backing vegetation where locally present;
- shallow nearshore water with visible or hidden changes in bottom profile;
- long linear sightlines along some shores contrasted with enclosed coves and rock breaks.

### Movement and navigation

- shoreline travel is usually easy to orient but may be blocked by cliffs, headlands, channels or deep coves;
- firm wet sand, loose dry sand, cobble and slick rock create very different footing without requiring separate presets;
- crossing from land to water may be simple on a beach and difficult on a cliffed or broken shore;
- headlands and visible coastal landmarks support orientation while fog, glare or darkness can reduce that advantage;
- exact surf, tide and storm movement effects require later overlay/local authority.

### Hazards

- unstable sand, loose cobble or slick algae-covered rock;
- wave-washed surfaces and unexpectedly deep nearshore cuts;
- undercut banks, collapsing dune faces or falling rock where appropriate;
- exposure to wind, spray and limited shelter on open strands;
- stranded debris, hooks, broken structures or sharp natural material;
- severe surf, storm surge, rip currents, extreme heat/cold or contamination are not assumed baseline effects.

### Encounters and challenges

Use blocked shore routes, stranded cargo or drift material, hidden coves, signs of feeding or nesting activity, temporary camps, fishing/harvesting activity, washed-up objects, damaged docks or markers, difficult land-water transitions, and decisions between exposed shoreline travel and slower inland detours.

### Rest and shelter

Coves, dune hollows above ordinary wash lines, rock overhangs, drift shelters, vegetation-backed ground and nearby built structures can provide cover. Low beaches, isolated bars and caves with uncertain water reach are poor choices without local tide/weather knowledge.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A long open strand ends abruptly at a rocky headland.
2. Fresh tracks and feeding signs follow the high-water line.
3. A large piece of drift material or cargo has washed ashore.
4. A quiet cove offers shelter but shows signs of recent use.
5. A shallow-looking crossing between rocks is deeper than expected.
6. Loose sand or cobble slows travel across an otherwise open route.
7. A damaged marker, pier or small landing reveals a formerly used access point.
8. Shorebirds or other small coastal life erupt suddenly from concealment.
9. An exposed rock shelf provides a shortcut with poor footing.
10. Debris gathered at the strand line conceals a useful or hazardous object.
11. Inland terrain forces travelers back toward the water edge.
12. A narrow beach corridor leaves little room between steep ground and the water.

## 2. Tidal Flats

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-TIDAL-FLATS`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-COASTAL`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-WETLAND`  
**Variants:** Mudflat; Sandflat; Tidal Flat

### Overview

Broad low coastal ground that alternates between exposure and inundation as water level changes. The defining baseline is the intertidal geometry: shallow channels, soft substrate, exposed bars, pools and long distances with little high ground. Exact tide schedules and surge severity are not authored here; they remain local or later hydrology/weather authority.

### Environmental features

- mud, sand or mixed sediment with variable bearing strength;
- shallow drainage channels, rivulets and water-filled depressions;
- isolated pools and exposed bars;
- shell beds, vegetation patches or low banks where locally appropriate;
- strong visual openness with subtle low-relief route obstacles;
- a clear distinction between reliable high ground and temporarily exposed flat.

### Movement and navigation

- direct-looking routes can become inefficient because of channels and deep mud;
- firmer ridges, bars and established paths may be much faster than straight-line travel;
- shallow water may be easy to cross while soft sediment beneath it remains hazardous;
- landmarks can be scarce across broad flats, making distant high ground important for orientation;
- changing water level can alter route availability, but exact timing must come from local/tidal authority.

### Hazards

- deep mud or suction-prone sediment;
- concealed channels and sudden soft spots;
- becoming isolated from high ground as water returns;
- glare, wind and exposure across open flats;
- unstable footing around pools and channel edges;
- contamination, severe weather or dangerous tide rates require explicit later authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use route races to higher ground, stranded equipment, shellfish or other resource gathering, feeding concentrations, temporary pools, hidden channels, local harvesters, exposed wreckage, buried objects, and disagreements over whether a shortcut will remain passable.

### Rest and shelter

Reliable rest requires high ground, stable banks, built platforms or clearly safe shoreline above ordinary water reach. Broad exposed flat, isolated bars and low islands are unsuitable for long rests unless local water-level knowledge establishes safety.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A firm-looking shortcut gives way to knee-deep mud.
2. A narrow tidal channel forces a long detour or careful crossing.
3. A dense concentration of small feeding life marks a productive pool.
4. A stranded boat, crate or structure rests far from the present waterline.
5. Distant high ground becomes the obvious safe objective.
6. A line of stakes, markers or old posts indicates a traditional route.
7. A broad reflective pool hides irregular footing beneath shallow water.
8. Fresh disturbance shows that something large crossed the flat recently.
9. A sand or shell ridge provides fast travel above surrounding mud.
10. Windblown sediment or spray reduces visibility across the open flat.
11. A buried object is exposed by erosion along a shallow channel.
12. The easiest path ahead lies through ground that will be vulnerable if water level rises.

## 3. Coral Reef

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-CORAL-REEF`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-AQUATIC-STRUCTURE`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-SUBMERGED`  
**Variants:** Fringing Reef; Barrier Reef; Patch Reef

### Overview

A submerged structured marine environment where rigid biological/mineral growth creates walls, ledges, channels, arches, towers, pockets and narrow passages. The key gameplay distinction is dense three-dimensional route structure within water, not any specific real-world species list. Depth, water clarity, current, temperature and reef ecology remain local parameters or later Habitat Signature/overlay authority.

### Environmental features

- branching, plate-like, massive or broken structural forms creating complex relief;
- channels, gullies, overhangs, arches and sheltered pockets;
- open water lanes alternating with dense structure;
- sand patches, rubble fields or bare rock between structured zones;
- strong vertical layering from bottom structure toward upper water;
- fragile surfaces or unstable broken sections where local conditions support them.

### Movement and navigation

- direct movement is often blocked by structure even when the destination is visible;
- narrow gaps may admit swimmers or small bodies while excluding larger vehicles or mounts, whose rules remain external;
- vertical movement over or under structure is as important as horizontal movement;
- channels can create fast routes, dead ends or sheltered approaches;
- persistent formations provide navigation landmarks but dense similarity can still cause disorientation.

### Hazards

- collision with hard or sharp structure;
- entrapment in narrow gaps or collapsing rubble;
- reduced line-of-sight around dense formations;
- local current acceleration through channels and gaps;
- fragile structure that can be damaged by careless movement;
- dangerous pressure, severe storms, contamination or specific hostile creatures require later authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use hidden passages, feeding disturbances, territorial signs, damaged or unusually barren reef sections, trapped debris, salvage lodged in structure, narrow-route decisions, observation points, resource patches, and choices between exposed open water and concealed structured routes.

### Rest and shelter

Sheltered pockets, lee sides of large formations, sandy bottoms and enclosed cavities can provide temporary protection from ordinary movement and current. A secure long rest still depends on breathing, pressure, equipment and participant needs owned by external systems.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A narrow channel opens into a surprisingly large sheltered basin.
2. Dense structure blocks the direct route and forces a vertical detour.
3. A burst of small marine life reveals hidden feeding activity nearby.
4. Broken reef material has collapsed across a familiar passage.
5. A piece of salvage or cargo is wedged deep inside branching structure.
6. An unusually barren patch interrupts otherwise dense growth.
7. A current accelerates sharply through an arch or constriction.
8. A high formation provides an excellent observation point but little cover on approach.
9. A deep crevice contains signs of repeated occupation.
10. Fine sediment suddenly clouds the water after nearby disturbance.
11. Two channels lead around the same formation, one open and one concealed.
12. Fragile structure must be crossed or bypassed without causing damage.

## 4. Kelp Forest

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-KELP-FOREST`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-AQUATIC-STRUCTURE`  
**Secondary archetype:** `ARCH-SUBMERGED`  
**Variants:** Kelp Forest; Dense Marine Vegetation

### Overview

A submerged environment dominated by tall flexible vegetation that creates a layered three-dimensional canopy, vertical stalk corridors, drifting cover and changing sightlines. It shares dense-structure navigation with reefs but differs in flexibility, entanglement potential, movement with current and stronger vertical canopy behavior.

### Environmental features

- tall anchored vegetation extending upward through much of the water column;
- dense stands broken by open lanes and clearings;
- upper canopy, mid-water stalk zones and bottom-level holdfast/rock areas;
- drifting fronds that change local visibility and route width;
- sheltered pockets where dense growth reduces ordinary water movement;
- detached vegetation or floating mats where locally appropriate.

### Movement and navigation

- travel through dense stands requires route choice around stalks and fronds;
- clearings and natural lanes become preferred corridors;
- vertical travel may pass above, within or below the densest canopy layer;
- flexible vegetation can close behind a traveler and obscure the route back;
- currents may bend vegetation consistently, providing orientation clues while also narrowing passages.

### Hazards

- entanglement in dense vegetation or drifting material;
- sudden loss of visibility as fronds move across sightlines;
- collision with rock, holdfasts or hidden structure near the bottom;
- disorientation in visually repetitive stands;
- stronger local movement where vegetation parts or channels water;
- pressure, severe temperature, storms and specific creatures remain later/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use hidden clearings, entangled objects, feeding or nesting signs, damaged vegetation corridors, sudden movement in the canopy, resource gathering, route markers tied to stalks, concealed camps or structures, and pursuit/avoidance through different vertical layers.

### Rest and shelter

Dense vegetation can break ordinary current and conceal a temporary resting area, while bottom rock shelves or clearings may offer stable positions. Long-rest viability still depends on breathing, equipment and participant needs outside ENV authority.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A narrow clear lane cuts through otherwise dense growth.
2. Detached vegetation drifts past carrying debris or a useful object.
3. Fronds suddenly fold in one direction, revealing a stronger current.
4. An open clearing shows signs of repeated feeding or occupation.
5. Dense stalks conceal a rock wall until travelers are close to it.
6. A route marker or tied object appears on a prominent stalk.
7. Fine sediment rises from the bottom after unseen movement.
8. A large patch of damaged or uprooted vegetation creates an exposed corridor.
9. An entangled object hangs above the bottom and shifts with the water.
10. The canopy becomes so dense that vertical movement is easier than horizontal travel.
11. A sheltered pocket is unusually still compared with the surrounding water.
12. The apparent route back has vanished behind overlapping vegetation.

## 5. Deep Ocean / Abyssal

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-DEEP-OCEAN`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-SUBMERGED`  
**Variants:** Deep Ocean; Abyssal Water Column

### Overview

A deep open-water environment far below ordinary surface influence, defined by immense three-dimensional space, limited fixed landmarks, isolation and depth-dependent physical conditions. The preset does not itself assign a universal pressure penalty, darkness rule or temperature band; those belong to ENV-12/local parameters so the same structure can support different worlds.

### Environmental features

- vast open water with few nearby fixed landmarks;
- vertical depth as a primary navigation dimension;
- suspended particles, drifting organic material or local clouds where applicable;
- distant seafloor or no visible bottom depending on depth;
- isolated ridges, vents, debris or structures as rare navigation anchors;
- strong separation between local water column and remote surface access.

### Movement and navigation

- navigation may depend on instruments, vertical reference, current direction or rare landmarks rather than visible terrain;
- movement is fully three-dimensional with little natural restriction on horizontal route choice;
- losing orientation can separate travelers from intended depth as well as horizontal position;
- long distances between shelter or fixed structure increase exposure;
- exact swimming, vehicle and pressure-performance rules remain external.

### Hazards

- disorientation in open three-dimensional space;
- extreme distance from shelter, surface access or reliable landmarks;
- concealed currents or vertical water movement;
- debris or suspended material detected only at close range;
- equipment failure becoming more consequential because of isolation;
- darkness, crushing pressure, cold, toxic water or supernatural effects require explicit ENV-12/13/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use drifting debris, distant lights or signals, unexplained movement in open water, lost equipment, isolated structures, vertical navigation errors, resource or sensor anomalies, sudden current changes and decisions about whether to approach rare landmarks or remain in open water.

### Rest and shelter

Open deep water provides little natural shelter. Stable seafloor features, large structures, vehicles, caves or engineered habitats may provide secure positions, but all breathing, pressure and life-support requirements remain owned by external systems.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A distant moving light or signal appears with no obvious landmark nearby.
2. A slow current has carried the group farther vertically than expected.
3. Suspended debris indicates a structure or event somewhere up-current.
4. A large shadow crosses far beyond reliable identification range.
5. A rare rock outcrop or artificial object becomes the only fixed landmark in view.
6. Fine particles suddenly thicken and reduce already limited visibility.
7. A dropped or drifting object continues sinking beyond easy recovery.
8. Instruments or visual references disagree about orientation.
9. A vertical layer of water behaves differently from the water above and below it.
10. A faint sound, vibration or disturbance travels from an unknown direction.
11. The planned route crosses an unexpectedly empty region with no shelter or landmarks.
12. A patch of concentrated life or material appears in otherwise sparse open water.

## 6. Ocean Trench

**Preset:** `ENVPRESET-MAR-OCEAN-TRENCH`  
**Primary archetype:** `ARCH-SUBMERGED`  
**Variants:** Ocean Trench; Hadal Trench

### Overview

A deep submerged environment organized around a long narrow depression, steep walls, terraces, slopes and a confined deep axis. Unlike generic deep open water, trench topography restores strong terrain orientation and creates vertical barriers, chokepoints, wall-following routes and isolated basins. The trench remains a preset-level topographic specialization of Submerged rather than a new archetype.

### Environmental features

- steep walls, scarps, ledges and terraces;
- a deep axial floor or chain of basins;
- side gullies, wall recesses and sediment slopes;
- rockfall or debris fields where local geology supports them;
- strong vertical relief producing clear above/below route relationships;
- isolated vents, seeps, deposits or structures where locally authored.

### Movement and navigation

- following the trench axis provides strong directional orientation;
- crossing from one wall to the other may require substantial open-water travel;
- ledges and terraces create staging points but can terminate abruptly;
- steep walls constrain direct ascent/descent and produce route chokepoints;
- side gullies may offer shortcuts, concealment or dead ends;
- exact current, pressure and vehicle handling rules remain later/external authority.

### Hazards

- steep drops and difficult vertical escape routes;
- unstable slopes, falling debris or sediment movement;
- confined route choices along walls and floor;
- disorientation when leaving terrain reference for open water above the trench;
- isolation from surface or shallow-water support;
- geologic activity, extreme pressure, darkness, cold or toxic vent chemistry require explicit later/local authority.

### Encounters and challenges

Use blocked wall routes, unstable ledges, signs of activity near seeps or vents, lost equipment on slopes, rare shelters, narrow gullies, ancient debris, route choices between exposed axial travel and wall-hugging movement, and discoveries revealed by fresh sediment movement.

### Rest and shelter

Ledges, wall recesses, stable basin margins, caves or engineered structures may provide positional shelter. Open trench floor and unstable slopes are poor rest sites. Life-support and pressure survival remain external concerns.

### Random encounters (d12)

1. A narrow ledge ends at a sheer wall drop.
2. Fresh sediment movement has uncovered an object or structure.
3. A side gully appears to bypass a blocked section of wall.
4. Falling debris forces travelers away from the wall into open water.
5. A sheltered recess contains signs of repeated occupation.
6. A distant glow, vent or disturbance marks a point on the trench floor.
7. A slope gives way and sends sediment cascading downward.
8. The trench narrows into a natural chokepoint.
9. A terrace offers a stable route but lies far above the axial floor.
10. Old wreckage or debris has collected against a wall feature.
11. A deep basin interrupts the otherwise continuous trench floor.
12. Leaving the wall for a direct crossing removes nearly all nearby visual reference.
